An automated market maker normally prices only from its own reserves, so it trails the real market and pays arbitrageurs to catch up. This paper lets the pool read a price oracle too. It proves that any well-behaved oracle-priced pool quotes a weighted average of the oracle price and its own inventory price, then works out when leaning on the oracle pays. Turn the dial at the top. §2.2 Theorem 2.8 · §2.3 Example 2.11

Every oracle-priced pool is a weighted average

The pool has two anchors. The oracle price π comes from outside. The inventory price π* comes from the reserves alone: it is the quote at which pool and oracle would agree.

The paper proves that if the quote never passes on the whole of an oracle move, it always sits between the two anchors, a share λ of the way toward the oracle. Drag either anchor.

p = λ π + (1 − λ) π*Theorem 2.8

Leaning on the oracle also concentrates liquidity. With a correct oracle, depth at the market price is 1/(1 − λ) times a CPMM of the same value. When the oracle is wrong, depth at the true price falls away, and past a break-even error the pool ends up shallower than the CPMM.

Trading lag risk for oracle risk

Liquidity providers lose to arbitrageurs whenever the quote is stale. The paper's loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) formula splits that loss into two parts: the market move the quote misses, weighted by (1 − λ)², and the oracle's own error, weighted by λ².

ℓ / V = ⅛ · depth · [(1 − λ)² σ² + λ² ση²]Remark 3.2

Each step toward the oracle trims lag but imports noise, and the extra depth amplifies whatever is left. With noise ratio α = ση/σ, the pool beats a CPMM in the same state only while λ < 1/(1 + α²).

§3.2.1 Proposition 3.11 · Remark 3.13 · Lemma 3.14

Sandwiching an oracle update

A push oracle posts each update as its own transaction. An attacker can buy just before the update, on the stale curve, and sell just after, on the refreshed curve at the higher price.

The pool starts with 1 unit of each asset at a price of 1, so it is worth 2. The oracle then jumps from 0 to Δπ. In the paper's example (λ = 0.6, Δπ = 0.5), plain arbitrage after the update takes 2.7% of the pool. Draining the risky reserve first takes 25.6%.

The price-tracking design (λ = 1) is worse: its curve never touches the axes, so the take keeps rising toward the whole pool as the front-run grows.

A fee on each leg closes the gap. Once λ·Δπ ≤ −log(1 − γ), front-running adds nothing, and the update itself offers no arbitrage.

What a year of SPY quotes says

The authors replay every second of SPY's 2023 national best bid and offer against a simulated tokenized-SPY pool. Arbitrageurs are the only traders, each day starts fresh at the open, and they sweep λ from 0 to 0.99 and fees from 0 to 50 bps under three oracle setups.
